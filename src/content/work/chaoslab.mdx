<Section n="01" title="Why build it">

Chaos engineering is easy to explain and hard to feel. "Deliberately break production to learn how it fails" sounds reckless until you see that the alternative is learning during a real outage. ChaosLab makes the loop visible: define steady state, form a hypothesis, inject a fault, detect it, contain the cascade, recover.

<Aside>The first principle of chaos engineering: build a hypothesis around steady-state behaviour, then try to disprove it.</Aside>
</Section>

<Section n="02" title="The system under test">

A Netflix-like architecture: an API gateway in front of user, auth and content-catalogue services, a recommendation engine, a CDN edge, a primary database and a Redis cache, spread across zones A and B. The simulator renders the topology on a canvas with traffic particles flowing along each dependency, and tracks health, latency (p50/p95/p99), error rate and throughput against a baseline of 12 ms, 0.1% errors and 1,240 requests a second.

<Figure src={steady} alt="ChaosLab in steady state: eight healthy services, health 100, flat latency and error sparklines" caption="Steady state: every service green, health 100." />
</Section>

<Section n="03" title="Faults, cascades and recovery">

- **Six experiments,** each with its own impact model: instance kill, latency injection (3–18× normal latency), network partition (cuts every edge to a service), resource exhaustion, cascade failure and zone outage.
- **Cascades:** a severe fault, a cascade experiment or a zone outage degrades every direct dependency of the failed service. The spread stops there, which is the containment the hypothesis is testing.
- **Recovery** is staged: failed services turn blue (recovering) and come back one at a time, 800 ms apart, while metrics ease back to baseline instead of snapping.
- **Chaos Monkey mode** keeps injecting random faults on an interval, never two at once, with auto-recovery between them.

<Figure src={injected} alt="ChaosLab during a chaos injection: a failed node with a red cross, degraded neighbours, health down and errors spiking" caption="Mid-experiment: one service down, its neighbours degraded, health falling." />
</Section>

<Section n="04" title="What I'd change">

- Model replicas, so killing one instance of a replicated service is survivable and the difference shows.
- Add SLOs and error budgets, so an experiment passes or fails against a stated objective.
- Export experiment runs, so results can be compared across topology changes.

</Section>
